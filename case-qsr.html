A pricing recommendation engine for QSR franchises.

Built a menu-level pricing recommendation system that helped franchise operators set item prices using inventory cost movements, local competitive signals, and margin targets — packaged so a non-technical store manager could see the suggested price, the reasoning, and the trade-off in a single view.

Client
QSR brand with multi-franchise footprint (Deloitte engagement)
Role
Data Analyst & Modeler
Timeline
2024
Focus
Modeling · Decision Support · Pricing
§ Overview

Pricing as a recurring decision, not an annual exercise.

QSR menu pricing is deceptively hard. Input costs move week to week. Competitive pressure varies by trade area. Franchisees have margin and traffic targets that don't always pull in the same direction. And the people making the actual pricing decision are store and area managers — not pricing analysts.

The brand had been running pricing as a once- or twice-a-year exercise driven from corporate. The result was the familiar one: prices that were either stale relative to cost movements or out of line with local competitive reality. Margin leaked quietly. Where prices were raised, traffic dipped without a clear sense of why.

What the engagement needed to produce:

  • A recommendation engine that produced a defensible price per menu item per trade area, refreshed at a meaningful cadence rather than annually.
  • Inputs that reflected the real drivers: ingredient cost trajectories, local competitive prices where available, item-level demand elasticity estimates, and margin targets.
  • An interface that surfaced the why behind a recommendation, not just the number — a store manager needed to be able to override with their own context.
  • A system that learned from accepted versus overridden recommendations over time.
§ The Solution

Predictions plus the reasoning, in one view.

The recommendation system was built as a sequence of models layered onto a clean transactional and operational dataset, all stitched together on AWS infrastructure with Python services and SQL feature pipelines.

Demand elasticity was estimated per item per trade-area cluster using historical transaction data, with controls for promotions, weather, weekday effects, and seasonality. Items with thin data were borrowed-strength estimated against the closest comparable items rather than ignored.

Cost and margin logic was modeled as a deterministic engine on top of the demand model: ingredient cost feeds, packaging and labor allocations, and franchise margin targets fed a constrained optimization that produced a recommended price range for each item.

Competitive signal was incorporated where data was reliable — competitor menu pricing snapshots were pulled and joined at the trade-area level, with confidence weighting so weak signals didn't dominate the recommendation.

The franchisee interface was the actual product. A Tableau-based front end showed each item with its current price, the recommended price, the expected demand and margin impact, and the three or four reasons driving the recommendation — cost movement, competitor benchmark, elasticity, target margin. Managers could accept, override, or defer, and overrides were logged as feedback for the next model retrain.

Backtesting was disciplined. Recommendations were validated against held-out historical periods before deployment, with attention to whether the model's accepted recommendations would have improved margin versus baseline pricing — not just whether the predictions were statistically accurate.

§ Results

A pricing decision franchisees could defend.

The win wasn't a single number; it was that pricing stopped being an opaque corporate edict and became a transparent local decision with backing.

  • Pricing cadence shifted from once or twice a year to a regular review rhythm with fresh inputs, narrowing the lag between cost movement and price response.
  • Recommendations were adopted at a healthy rate precisely because the reasoning was visible. Overrides, when they happened, were informed — and they became signal for the next iteration of the model.
  • Margin protection improved on the items where elasticity estimates were strongest, without the traffic dips that come from blunt across-the-board price increases.
  • Franchisee trust in corporate pricing guidance increased, because the system showed its work.
  • The model improved over time by learning from accepted versus overridden recommendations — the system got more useful the longer it ran.
