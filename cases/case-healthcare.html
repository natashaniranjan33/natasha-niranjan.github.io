Healthcare & Life Sciences Data Engineering Migration & Automation Python SQL AWS

Migrating and automating a healthcare data estate, without losing trust in the data.

Re-platformed a healthcare client's reporting and analytics environment by building automated migration, transformation, and validation pipelines in Python and SQL — moving from a fragmented, manually-reconciled state to a governed, auditable data layer the team could actually rely on.

Client
Healthcare provider (Deloitte engagement)
Role

§ Overview

A data estate too large to move by hand.

The client's analytics environment had grown organically across legacy systems — patient records, claims data, operational reporting, and a long tail of CSV exports the team had been stitching together for years.

A platform consolidation was overdue. The risk was not the move itself; it was what tends to break during a migration: silent column-type drift, lost referential integrity between fact and dimension tables, dates that quietly shift timezone, and PII fields that survive the move but lose their access controls along the way. Trust in the data was the entire deliverable.

Goals for the engagement:

  • Migrate data from legacy sources into a unified, governed warehouse without manual reconciliation.
  • Automate transformation logic so business rules lived in version-controlled code, not in someone's saved query.
  • Build validation that ran during the migration, not after — row counts, schema parity, referential integrity, and HIPAA-relevant field masking.
  • Leave behind documentation and tests the client team could maintain on their own.
§ The Solution

Code-first migration, tested every step of the way.

I built the migration as a series of idempotent Python pipelines orchestrated against AWS infrastructure (S3 for staged extracts, Lambda for event-driven steps, EC2 for longer-running transformation jobs). Each pipeline handled a single source-to-target movement with three explicit phases: extract, transform, validate.

Transformations lived in SQL where they belonged. Heavy logic — joins across patient, encounter, and claims tables; slowly-changing dimensions; deduplication on probabilistic identity keys — ran as parameterized SQL templates rendered from Python. Lightweight orchestration, logging, and validation stayed in Python. This split made the business logic readable for the client's analysts and the orchestration testable for the engineering team.

Validation ran inline. Each migration step emitted assertion checks before committing: row-count parity within tolerance, schema-type matches, primary-key uniqueness, foreign-key integrity, and PHI/PII field-level masking confirmations. A failed assertion halted the pipeline and surfaced a structured error rather than corrupting downstream tables.

Idempotency was the design rule. Every pipeline could be re-run safely. This sounds boring; it's the single most important property in a real migration, because something always needs to be re-run.

Reporting consumers downstream — Tableau and Power BI — were pointed at the new layer with parallel runs against the legacy source until parity was confirmed for at least a full reporting cycle.

§ Results

A migration that nobody had to babysit.

The most useful outcome was the one that's hard to put a number on: the client's analyst team stopped spending mornings reconciling reports against legacy sources.

  • Manual reconciliation effort dropped substantially. Tasks that previously required side-by-side spreadsheet comparison were replaced by automated validation reports.
  • Migration cycle time shortened. Re-runs that previously took analyst hours of attention completed unattended overnight.
  • Data lineage became visible. Each row in the new warehouse traced back through transformation steps to a known source, with timestamps and validation outcomes captured.
  • PHI handling tightened. Field-level masking and access controls were enforced at the pipeline layer rather than relying on downstream report-level filters.
  • The client team could maintain it. Documentation, test suites, and a runbook were handed off — and the system kept running after the engagement ended, which is the real test.
