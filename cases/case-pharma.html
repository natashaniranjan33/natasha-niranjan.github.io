Building the analytics layer for a $30B pharma supply chain.

Designed and shipped the end-to-end data layer that monitored manufacturing yield, logistics, transportation, drug testing, and distribution KPIs — turning fragmented operational feeds into a single source of truth the supply chain leadership could plan against.

Client
Global pharmaceutical manufacturer (Deloitte engagement)
Role
Data Analyst · AI & Analytics Consultant
Timeline
2023 – 2025
Focus
Pipelines · Modeling · Reporting
§ Overview

Operations data that nobody trusted in the same way twice.

A pharma supply chain spanning manufacturing plants, contract testing labs, distribution centers, and last-mile logistics partners produced more operational data than the analytics team could keep up with.

The problem wasn't that data didn't exist; it was that the same KPI was calculated three different ways depending on who pulled the report. Yield numbers from manufacturing didn't reconcile with batch-release counts from QA. Logistics on-time-in-full diverged between the warehouse system and the freight partner feed. By the time these reached the leadership dashboard, the numbers had been quietly massaged into agreement, and nobody could tell you exactly how.

The engagement scope:

  • Stand up a governed analytics layer covering manufacturing yield, batch release, logistics performance, transportation cost, and distribution fulfillment.
  • Define KPIs once, with logic written in code and reviewed by operations leads, so every dashboard derived from the same definitions.
  • Make root-cause investigation tractable: when a yield dip showed up, the analyst should be able to trace it to a plant, line, shift, and batch in minutes, not days.
  • Support production planning and scenario analysis with forecasting models that operations leadership could actually defend.
§ The Solution

Pipelines, semantic definitions, and the discipline of a single source.

I built the analytics layer on Databricks and Google BigQuery, with AWS-orchestrated ingestion (S3 staging, EC2 transformation jobs, Lambda triggers) pulling from manufacturing execution systems, ERP exports, QA batch records, logistics partner feeds, and distribution center scans.

SQL did the heavy lifting for the transformation layer — multi-stage models with explicit conformed dimensions for plant, line, product, batch, lot, and shipment. Python handled orchestration, data quality assertions, and forecasting model training.

Every KPI had one definition, in one place. Yield, OEE, OTIF, batch-release cycle time, transportation cost per unit — each codified as a versioned SQL model with a tested logic spec. Dashboards consumed these models; they did not redefine them. When a definition needed to change, the change went through review, and every downstream report inherited it automatically.

Forecasting served planning, not vanity. Demand and yield forecasts were built with feature engineering grounded in operational reality (seasonality, campaign cadence, batch size effects, line speed history) rather than off-the-shelf time-series tooling. Outputs included prediction intervals and explainability artifacts so operations leaders could pressure-test assumptions instead of trusting a black box.

Reporting met people where they worked. Tableau and Power BI dashboards with drill-down across the conformed dimensions made it possible to start at a global KPI and arrive at the responsible batch, line, or shipment in a handful of clicks.

§ Results

A supply chain leadership team that argued about decisions, not numbers.

The most useful shift was cultural: the weekly operations review stopped opening with twenty minutes of "whose number is right?" and started opening with what the numbers meant.

  • KPI alignment across functions. Manufacturing, QA, logistics, and distribution worked from the same definitions — disagreements moved from "is this number correct" to "what should we do about it."
  • Root-cause investigation accelerated. The conformed-dimension model meant a yield anomaly could be traced to plant, line, shift, and batch directly from the executive dashboard, without ad-hoc SQL.
  • Pipeline processing time and analyst manual effort reduced materially (~30% and ~40% respectively for the engagements I led), freeing the analytics team to spend time on investigation rather than data wrangling.
  • Forecasts that operations leaders trusted. Explainable yield and demand models with prediction intervals were adopted into the weekly S&OP rhythm rather than parked.
  • Knowledge transfer that stuck. Documentation, semantic-model specs, and runbooks were handed off; the client's internal team owned the layer after engagement close.
