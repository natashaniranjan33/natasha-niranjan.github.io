Marketing analytics for an e-commerce retailer.

Stood up the marketing analytics layer for a retail e-commerce client — channel performance, acquisition cost, attribution across paid and organic touchpoints, and customer lifetime value — so that marketing spend decisions sat on a defensible data foundation rather than channel-by-channel platform numbers.

Client
E-commerce retailer (Deloitte engagement)
Role
Data Analyst & Marketing Analytics Consultant
Timeline
2023 – 2024
Focus
Attribution · Modeling · Reporting
§ Overview

Every channel claimed credit for the same conversion.

The marketing team had platform-level numbers from every channel — paid search, paid social, affiliate, email, organic — and each platform happily reported the conversions it touched. Added together, the platforms claimed roughly twice the actual revenue. Nobody had a single trustworthy view of which channel was actually driving incremental business, which meant every budget conversation was an argument backed by selectively favorable screenshots.

The engagement was about building the data layer underneath those conversations:

  • A unified customer-and-event model that tied marketing touchpoints to actual orders and revenue at the customer level, not the platform level.
  • Attribution logic that produced defensible channel and campaign contribution numbers — multi-touch by default, with the option to compare against simpler last-touch or position-based models.
  • Customer lifetime value modeling so acquisition decisions could be evaluated against the right horizon, not first-order revenue alone.
  • Reporting the marketing team could actually use day-to-day, plus a clean data foundation for downstream experimentation.
§ The Solution

An event-grain customer model the whole team could query.

I built the underlying data model on AWS-orchestrated Python and SQL pipelines, ingesting from web analytics, ad platform exports, the order system, email platform, and CRM. Everything resolved to a single customer key, with marketing touchpoints joined as time-stamped events against orders and revenue.

Identity resolution was the unglamorous prerequisite. Anonymous-to-known stitching across cookies, device IDs, hashed emails, and order events was handled as a deliberate, testable layer — because every downstream attribution number was only as good as the joins underneath it.

Attribution wasn't a single number. The model layer produced last-touch, first-touch, linear, time-decay, and position-based attributions in parallel, alongside a more rigorous multi-touch attribution model. Reporting surfaced the spread between models so stakeholders could see where channel credit was robust versus where it depended on assumptions.

Customer lifetime value was estimated using historical cohort revenue curves, with cohort segmentation by acquisition channel and first-product category. This let acquisition cost decisions be evaluated against expected long-term value, not just first-order economics.

The dashboards were built around questions, not metrics. Channel performance, cohort decay, campaign incrementality, funnel diagnostics — each view started from a real question a marketer would ask and worked backward to what should be on the screen.

§ Results

Budget conversations backed by one set of numbers.

The result was less about uplift in any single campaign and more about the marketing team being able to make decisions from the same data, with shared definitions.

  • A single customer-level event model replaced platform-by-platform reconciliation as the basis for marketing analysis.
  • Multi-model attribution surfaced where channel credit was real versus assumed, sharpening the case for and against specific channel investments.
  • Lifetime-value-aware acquisition decisions reframed conversations about high-CAC channels that had previously looked unprofitable on a first-order basis.
  • Cohort-level retention diagnostics gave the lifecycle team a structured way to identify where churn was concentrated and which acquisition cohorts were worth doubling down on.
  • Self-serve reporting meant the marketing team stopped routing every question through analytics — and the analytics team got time back for higher-leverage work.
